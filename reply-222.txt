The jam narrative findings. The core tension, stated by narrative mentor John Ryan advising around forty jam teams: many fast-moving parts can be cut last-second and "pull apart your narrative," so think strategically; his first rule is "know your scope and fit your story inside your scope." His six vital pre-writing questions: core gameplay loop, player fantasy, best story complementing the loop, honest week-capacity, how success and fail states carry narrative, how much story without bogging gameplay or blowing the deadline. Plus the killer question: "Does this game need dialogue at all?" — dialogue last, not first. David Kuelz's Tip number one: "Story Starts With Gameplay" — the classic mistake is writing a story unrelated to second-by-second play and sliding it between chunks. His technique is "verbing" — list everything the player can do, move, attack, overheat, die, and build the story so gameplay is an insight into the character. And the LD submission realities: finish with a hard internal deadline two hours before submission; Theme, Audio, and Humor sit alongside Fun — narrative choices directly serve the Theme score and the Audio and Humor scores if barks land.

Validation — the final narrative checklist. From Mark Slabinski's eight principles of game dialogue: concision — he demonstrates a sixty-two-word line rewritten to twenty without losing meaning; every line must survive radical shortening. "The Treachery of Forced Lore" — lore is garnish, not the meal. "Barks — the deadly art": don't use barks as a crutch for what art and animation already communicate; always know each bark's purpose. And ninety-five percent of players won't remember a line a week later — favor lines that work in the moment. From PlayablePlan's string-freeze guide: a proper freeze has TWO boundaries, not one — a translation baseline with approved lines, and a release lock where only showstoppers pass. Every late change gets triaged with three questions: player impact, translation and voice impact, proof the current line is wrong. Four outcomes: DEFER, typo-verify, retranslate, or HOLD. Keep one change record per edit. From Sarah Beaulieu's bark-writing guide: three bark families — combat barks, fight barks tied to specific moments, situational lines; best practice is a voice matrix per character with one-line voice descriptions so names can be hidden and voices still differ; write subtext; solve repetition with enough variants. From Vois: separate informational barks from personality lines — an enemy warning must stay legible during combat.

The techniques — freeze-safe narrative polish. One: writing-as-feedback placement — never interrupt mid-action; place lines as feedback after a verb completes, limb destroyed then bark, and as reward after hard challenges; this is exactly our floaters plus aria-live plus ducking pattern, and it's freeze-safe because it needs no new systems. Two: tell part of the story without words — once anything communicates a beat, intent icons, scorched arena, heat glow, delete the bark that says it too; cuts word count and freeze risk simultaneously. Three: voice matrix plus variants — lock per-archetype voice descriptors and two to three variants per trigger up front; variants are cheap, new triggers are expensive. Four: the informational and personality split — freeze informational lines first; personality lines are the only candidates for late tweaks. Five: baseline plus change record — version the Vois table like a localization baseline; any edit after freeze needs string ID, old to new, reason — no silent edits.

What to avoid. Narrative feature creep — Ryan's number-one jam trap. Story and gameplay mismatch — barks contradicting what the player is doing destroy both. Barks as visual crutch — if the intent icon already says it, a bark saying it again is noise. Forced lore dumps. Late "small" text edits breaking the mix — unversioned changes desync captions, aria-live, floaters.

Shipped examples. Gods Will Be Watching — built in seventy-two hours for LD26, took a silver medal, crowdfunded into a full six-chapter narrative adventure published by Devolver Digital; the jam version's entire narrative was one survival scenario around forced moral sacrifice — a single-scenario freeze that scaled later. The Lion's Song — a jam prototype expanded into a four-episode commercial adventure. Keep Talking and Nobody Explodes — born at Global Game Jam twenty-fourteen; the narrative IS the paper manual plus teammate chatter, freeze-safe by design because the story layer was deliberately decoupled from code. The pattern across all: each froze a tiny narrative surface and let mechanics carry the rest — exactly what our ten fixed triggers already embody.
