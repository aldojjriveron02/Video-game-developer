The jam hangar findings. Adam Thompson: set a personal deadline two hours before the real one — the submission hour is when things go wrong; use the buffer for fixes plus icon, screenshots, and a gameplay video. Enigma Dragons: two hours before jam end, create the game page and gameplay video; final hour, test the final build and submit once approved. PIGSquad budgets submitting at about eight percent of a jam — three hours of a forty-eight. The Survival Guide: install libraries, test exporting a small project, sign up for itch in advance — "you definitely don't want to be looking for an exporter during Submission Hour." Thompson: host web builds on itch.io, not Dropbox — bandwidth caps cut public downloads. Don't blank on audio — no audio is N/A or one-star in LD's Audio category, a significant hit to Overall. Post-jam: LD's sorting surfaces games by creator activity — play, rate, comment on other submissions; Thompson rates at least ten games a day.

Validation — the five-user protocol. Nielsen and Landauer's math: with an average per-user discovery rate of thirty-one percent, five users surface about eighty-five percent of usability problems. Better: multiple small tests — test, fix, retest. Three rounds of five beats one round of fifteen. NN/g session budgets: sixty to ninety minutes, overhead under eleven percent, focus on observing behavior not soliciting opinions. Bromley's guerrilla protocol: decide objectives as risk to objective to task — "a risk is something you think could be wrong"; find representative players; WATCH people play — watching two or three is more useful than surveying hundreds, and players can't report features they never discovered; trust observed behavior, use opinions only as clues. Recording: iOS built-in screen recording, Zoom, or OBS. For our hangar: five users, task scenarios like "build a legal mech and deploy," silent observation, then fix and retest. Gate metric: percent completing a legal deploy unassisted.

The techniques. Juice is a scoring category — the Survival Guide says judges can tell submissions with it from those without; add juice while adding features, not in a last-minute scramble. Final six hours: full playthrough — everyone plays through the game, tune visuals, fix bugs. For us: hangar to deploy to M3 duel on both desktop and iPhone 17. Submission assets: icon, screenshots, animated GIFs, gameplay video, customized itch page theme — the page is the first impression. Skeleton first: the first eight hours blitz to a playable skeleton from menu to victory. Our hangar's deploy gate is load-bearing structure, not polish. Hangar-specific last-mile: deploy-confirmation beat, actionable errors at the point of failure — that's MH-08 — instant stat preview on part swap, touch-target audit for iPhone 17, bfcache resync on mobile Safari.

What to avoid. The Draft trap — confirmed real case: a first-time jammer clicked "Submit your project" but left the itch project as a Draft; it was never actually submitted. Verify the page is published AND attached to the jam before the deadline. Exporting only at the end — one export at the end equals risk; upload builds as you work. The submission-hour stampede — everyone submits in the last hour and servers buckle; "on time is late, early is on time." Feature creep past the halfway mark — "Cut. Cut. Cut." Never scrap and restart mid-jam. Playtest traps: unrepresentative players, trusting what players say over what they do, helping too much, treating "I'd buy this" as evidence.
