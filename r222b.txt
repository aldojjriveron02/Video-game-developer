Part 2 of 3. Every late change gets triaged with three questions: player impact, translation and voice impact, proof the current line is wrong. Four outcomes: DEFER, typo-verify, retranslate, or HOLD. Keep one change record per edit. From Sarah Beaulieu's bark-writing guide: three bark families — combat barks, fight barks tied to specific moments, situational lines; best practice is a voice matrix per character with one-line voice descriptions so names can be hidden and voices still differ; write subtext; solve repetition with enough variants. From Vois: separate informational barks from personality lines — an enemy warning must stay legible during combat. The techniques — freeze-safe narrative polish. One: writing-as-feedback placement — never interrupt mid-action; place lines as feedback after a verb completes, limb destroyed then bark, and as reward after hard challenges; this is exactly our floaters plus aria-live plus ducking pattern, and it's freeze-safe because it needs no new systems. Two: tell part of the story without words — once anything communicates a beat, intent icons, scorched arena, heat glow, delete the bark that says it too; cuts word count and freeze risk simultaneously. Three: voice matrix plus variants — lock per-archetype voice descriptors and two to three variants per trigger up front; variants are cheap, new triggers are expensive. Four: the informational and personality split — freeze informational lines first; personality lines are the only candidates for late tweaks. Five: baseline plus change record — version the Vois table like a localization baseline; any edit after freeze needs string ID, old to new, reason — no silent edits. What to avoid. Narrative feature creep — Ryan's number-one jam trap. Story and gameplay mismatch — barks contradicting what the player is doing destroy both. Barks as visual crutch — if the intent icon already says it, a bark saying it again is noise. Forced lore dumps. Late "small" text edits breaking the mix — unversioned changes desync captions, aria-live, floaters. Shipped examples. Gods Will Be Watching — built in seventy-two hours for LD26, took a silver medal, crowdfunded into a full six-chapter narrative adventure published by Devolver Digital; the jam version's entire narrative was one survival scenario around forced moral sacrifice — a single-scenario freeze that scaled later.
