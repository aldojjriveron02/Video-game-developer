Full report is in your Drive. The jam narrative findings. The core tension, stated by narrative mentor John Ryan advising around forty jam teams: many fast-moving parts can be cut last-second and "pull apart your narrative," so think strategically; his first rule is "know your scope and fit your story inside your scope." His six vital pre-writing questions: core gameplay loop, player fantasy, best story complementing the loop, honest week-capacity, how success and fail states carry narrative, how much story without bogging gameplay or blowing the deadline. Plus the killer question: "Does this game need dialogue at all?" — dialogue last, not first. David Kuelz's Tip number one: "Story Starts With Gameplay" — the classic mistake is writing a story unrelated to second-by-second play and sliding it between chunks. His technique is "verbing" — list everything the player can do, move, attack, overheat, die, and build the story so gameplay is an insight into the character. And the LD submission realities: finish with a hard internal deadline two hours before submission; Theme, Audio, and Humor sit alongside Fun — narrative choices directly serve the Theme score and the Audio and Humor scores if barks land. Validation — the final narrative checklist. From Mark Slabinski's eight principles of game dialogue: concision — he demonstrates a sixty-two-word line rewritten to twenty without losing meaning; every line must survive radical shortening. "The Treachery of Forced Lore" — lore is garnish, not the meal. "Barks — the deadly art": don't use barks as a crutch for what art and animation already communicate; always know each bark's purpose. And ninety-five percent of players won't remember a line a week later — favor lines that work in the moment. From PlayablePlan's string-freeze guide: a proper freeze has TWO boundaries, not one — a translation baseline with approved lines, and a release lock where only showstoppers pass.
