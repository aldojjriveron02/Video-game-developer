Two hard-won specifics: IN-PERSON beats remote — without seeing the exact moments, feedback is uninterpretable; and add diegetic breadcrumbs — in-world hints that don't spell things out — "there can never be too many breadcrumbs." Nielsen's five-user math: model N times one minus one-minus-L to the n, typical L thirty-one percent; the curve bends at five users, after which "you are wasting your time by observing the same findings repeatedly." Better: THREE studies of five users than one study of fifteen. The second five-user round catches most of the remaining fifteen percent AND validates the fixes. This is the quantitative license for our gate — and it mandates a re-test round after fixes, not a single pass. The techniques — last-mile polish. The eight-word rule, sourced: George Fan's rule was NO MORE THAN EIGHT WORDS PER SENTENCE — via Hedges. Audit every tutorial text box against it. Pine's five invisible-tutorial methods: AFFORDANCES — design communicates use; SKILL GATES — block progress until the taught action is performed once, like Celeste's prologue freezing time until you dash; DEMONSTRATIVE — show don't tell, like Half-Life 2's barnacle eating a crow; TRIAL AND ERROR — safe failure with crystal-clear causality, like Spelunky; CONTEXT-SENSITIVE HINTS — Hollow Knight shows a hint only if the player stalls about five SECONDS at a skill gate. Gee's principle: "Information on Demand and Just in Time" — deliver each instruction at the moment of need, never before. What to avoid. Hedges' thirteen negative patterns: hand-holding, not skippable, patronizing tone, forced videos, information overload, ruins flow, irrelevant info, FAILED to provide necessary info, constant pauses, too long, unskippable popups, forced pace, repetitive. The text-wall trap: adding MORE tutorial text to fix confusion — fix the affordance or skill gate instead.
