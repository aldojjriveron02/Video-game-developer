The jam feel findings. The feel problem under a jam clock: game feel is built one tiny decision at a time. Jan Willem Nijman demonstrated this live in "The Art of Screenshake," applying around thirty small changes — bigger bullets, muzzle flash, hitstop, camera kick — to a deliberately boring shooter until it felt incredible. Feel can't be rushed, so the freeze discipline is: reserve the last six to twelve hours for polish and fluff only; implement zero major features on the final day; aim to have the game finished by Saturday night leaving Sunday for polish. Steve Swink's framework: game feel rests on five components — input response, visual feedback, audio feedback, camera behavior, animation timing. For a three-versus-three grid tactics game, real-time control is minimal by design, so the feel burden lands almost entirely on anticipation plus resolution feedback — telegraphs, hitstop, shake, floaters, barks. And the differentiator argument from two-time LD winner Chevy Ray: "Add visual flair. Barely anybody does this, so ninety-five percent of jam games tend to look like hard, dull prototypes… when an enemy dies, make it EXPLODE into particles everywhere, make buttons and interface elements pulse and move… Make everything alive."

Validation — the final feel checklist with specific numbers. From the GameCodex polish and juice checklist: the two-of-three pillars rule — every player action needs feedback from at least two of visual, audio, camera; priority order is screen shake, hitstop, particles, SFX, squash and stretch. Trauma-based shake: trauma zero to one, screen offset equals trauma squared, linear decay; light hit zero-point-one-five to zero-point-two-five at five per second; heavy zero-point-four to zero-point-six at three per second; explosion zero-point-seven to one at one-point-five per second; always offer reduce and disable for accessibility. Hitstop: light two frames, heavy four to five, boss five to eight, death freeze five to ten; keep input buffering and rendering running during hitstop. White flash on hit, one to two frames — called "the single most impactful visual juice technique." Particles: three to eight burst per impact, directional spread, fade out never pop out, pooled and recycled. Squash and stretch: plus-minus ten to twenty percent; always ease, never linear. UI: floating text zero-point-five to zero-point-eight seconds; transitions zero-point-three to zero-point-five seconds, no naked cuts. Audio: pitch variation plus-minus five to fifteen percent, layered SFX, ducking; "Audio is fifty percent of game feel and the most underinvested area." Kill slow-mo: zero-point-one to zero-point-two seconds at zero-point-three timescale. And the Before-After test: record ten seconds with all juice off versus on. From Easton Dev's game feel design article: the IEEE twenty-twenty-two framework's three highest-weight features are hit stop, sound coherence, and camera control; hitstop zero-point-zero-five to zero-point-one seconds; flash fifty to one-hundred milliseconds; shake amplitude two to three pixels weak, eight to ten strong; particles twenty to thirty count; floating text one to two seconds; the multimodal sequencing is vibration plus sound simultaneously, then flash at twenty milliseconds, particles at fifty, floating text at one hundred; the hundred-millisecond golden rule — any input must get visual or audio feedback within one hundred milliseconds or it feels delayed; frequency cap three to five feedbacks per second. From GameDesign.gg: the blindfold test, comparison test, mute test, frame-by-frame test, repetition test at three hundred reps; sound can make an action feel twenty to forty milliseconds faster; dynamic range matters — quiet moments make loud moments loud. From the turn-based juice thread: Buriedbornes' still portraits vibrate when attacking; screen shake "just the right amount at just the right time," starting with a little shake when the player commits a consequential decision; punchy SFX, "boost the bottom end"; hover micro-interactions with small bouncy animation plus subtle sound; characters talking on certain actions — which is our pilot-bark pattern; and the counterpoint — don't overdo it, short animations, never let the player wait for a transition. From Kenney's presentation checklist: subtract one to two hours from the deadline for presentation polish; unique title, never just the jam or theme name; first screenshot must not be a menu screen; short video or GIF with gameplay from the very first second; normalize audio; final full playthrough check.

The techniques — freeze-safe feel polish. Trauma-squared shake mapped to our existing three-state intensity. Hitstop with the input buffer kept alive — our buffer already exists. The sequencing layer: sound plus haptic on mobile, then flash at twenty milliseconds, particles at fifty, floating text at one hundred. "Helpful lies": cursor snap, selection forgiveness, skippable animations. Dynamic range: scale all feedback by intensity tiers with the frequency cap; silence before the VEX duel kill makes it land. Freeze-safe engineering: all juice parameters data-driven and tunable without code changes; the agnostic protocol as the single feel entry point; pilot-assist toggles as the post-freeze safety valve.

What to avoid. No new features in the polish window. Frib's rule: "You can waste hours tweaking insignificant things… When something is done and works, leave it like that unless you need to change something drastic." Hitstop that freezes input without a buffer. One-size-fits-all feedback with no dynamic range. The same ten seconds of audio loop. Animations that make the player wait. Naked scene cuts. Too many particles equals visual chaos. The ninety-five-percent trap is under-juicing, not over-juicing, at jam scale. Title equals theme name. Menu screenshot first. Last-minute submission — "Finish early. On time is late, early is on time."
