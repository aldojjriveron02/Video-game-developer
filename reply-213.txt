The jam narrative findings. LD judges score narrative directly: the official rules define MOOD as "Storytelling, emotion, and the vibe you get while playing" and HUMOR as "How amusing a game is. Humorous dialog, funny sounds." Our pilot barks sit exactly on the Humor category; our Mood targeting maps to the Mood category. This is a real competitive lever, not garnish. The ideal LD game "can be played right in the browser, in a few minutes, alone" — the submission-page narrative must get a judge from page-load to first input in under two minutes, with zero lore prerequisite. The submission page is the first narrative act: "people don't like to read" — page narrative must be visual and terse; the cited best example is Doki Doki Literature Club, which "brings its game world to its game page." GameDeveloper's three phases: preparation, execution, networking — finish two hours before the deadline; post-jam, rate about ten games a day. NarraScope's jam sticky: mechanics and hook FIRST, then write narrative to emphasize the mechanic; keep narrative under five thousand words for a two-week jam — proportionally far less for seventy-two hours.

Validation — the three-player one-shot specifics. Sample math: Nielsen's five users find eighty-five percent; MeasuringU's p of point-three-one. With three players, expected discovery is one minus point-six-nine cubed — about sixty-five percent. A defensible one-shot gate for a slice, but Koji says run two to three small rounds instead of one big one if a second evening exists. Bromley's protocol: recruit from the competitor set — mecha tactics and anime fans; one-to-one moderated, silent observation, no coaching; comprehension check with three open questions — "What is happening currently? Why is this happening? What do you expect to happen next?" — since mystery is core; appeal check asking "how interested are you in what happens next" regularly; pre-define the expected emotional response at each beat and capture whether players feel it.

The techniques — last-mile polish. Vois's bark checklist: write variants around trigger frequency and gameplay purpose; separate informational from personality; per-trigger audit of frequency, urgency, overlap, competing sounds; three variants for routine as a starting exercise; actionable info near the beginning; duration budget per trigger; test the line while doing the action; every line gets a specific intention; stable IDs; approve a small batch first. "Write the line the player needs, then give it permission to stay quiet." Beaulieu on bark craft: a bark's first function is to inform; two to ten variations per trigger; spread lore through universe-specific language like Orwell's Newspeak so even uncomprehended terms build immersion; characterize beyond faction — how does a shy versus annoyed character say hello; "narrative potential" — turn a bark into a dramatic question so the player fills gaps; a repeated bark can become a Proust's madeleine, like Age of Empires villagers — a gimmick can be a signature. His constraint ethic — "telling one or several information in only a few words" — is directly consonant with our eight-word rule.

What to avoid. Text walls before first input — violates the LD ideal and judges' reality. Repetition grating — Skyrim's "Never should have come here"; per Vois, "more lines don't fix excessive triggering," fix cooldowns and priority. Info-versus-personality blur — enemy warnings must stay legible; never let flavor bury the actionable word. Explaining the theme on the page instead of demonstrating it — judges reward the vibe, not the essay. Spending the two-hour buffer on new content. Post-jam ghosting — the networking phase is part of scoring well.
