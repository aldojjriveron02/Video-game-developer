Bromley on numbers: six players when discovering problems — recruit six so you still have five after no-shows; twelve when understanding players; a hundred when measuring. Citing Nielsen: five users surface about eighty percent of issues. "One player is infinitely more than none." The techniques — Okko's nine-point guide from his Cologne Game Lab thesis. One: a narrative scenario in which teaching makes sense. Two: a mentor character. Three: incorporate the first plot point into the tutorial. Four: reduce action possibilities — add mechanics one at a time. Five: a safe environment for mistakes, at the player's own pace. Six: minimal text — and dub it. Seven: decide what must be explained versus what the player discovers. Eight: different teaching modes per player, and make tutorials skippable. Nine: instructions only when relevant. His thesis game used an NPC pirate mentor that instructs only when the player seems to need it. George Fan's rule from Plants vs. Zombies: never show more than EIGHT WORDS on screen at any moment. Bergdahl's solutions: show don't tell; learn by doing; don't punish early mistakes; adaptive hints driven by actual player behavior; tutorials that are in-game, seamless, fun, and only as long as needed. GMTK on complex games: split the tutorial, spread it across the campaign, use affordances. What to avoid. Bergdahl's trap list: walls of text; breaking immersion; repeating the same tutorials; dull hologram training; non-canon alternate-reality training arenas; wasting time telling players things they already know; assuming the player's skill level.
