The jam feel findings. The gamestack checklist orders the final pass: control, then simulation, then forgiveness, then juice — "Don't juice a verb that doesn't yet feel right." Test-for criteria: input-to-pixel latency at or under fifty milliseconds for action at sixty fps on target hardware; a "mute plus no particles plus no screen effects" test — the stripped simulation must already feel acceptable before polish. Hit-stop from a per-event table, three to ten frames scaled to magnitude, with a cap on cumulative hit-stop per second. Trauma-based screen shake — accumulate and decay. Input buffer one hundred to two hundred milliseconds; coyote time four to six frames. All UI value changes eased, never linear; audio-video sync within fifty milliseconds; audio identifiable with the screen off; UI hover one to two hundred milliseconds, press squish under a hundred, transitions two to five hundred. The JUICE PACK starter numbers: hit-stop forty to eighty milliseconds, squash ten to twenty percent, pitch variance plus or minus ten percent. Kao's inverted-U: summed feedback should sit in the Medium to High band — Extreme hurts as much as None. Build a rarity ladder: reserve the top tier of each primitive — max shake, max hit-stop, slow-mo — for events happening once per session or less. itch's own Ludum Dare guide: judges download many games and close the page — "They're probably not going to be reading your instructions when they start your game" — so an intro and instruction screen IN the game is mandatory. Sound transforms a tech demo into a game. Breadth-first dev: don't spend all hours on one feature and end up with a tech demo.

Validation — the playtest protocol. gamedesignskills: plan one to eight specific questions per session; let the tester play without instructions first — "the first thirty seconds will tell you everything"; STAY SILENT until the tester asks for help; watch the tester's FACE, not the screen — facial expressions reveal feel; don't help or lead; keep a notebook log; ask at most three questions at the very end, open-ended — "what would you change," "how did it make you feel," rate zero to ten; "They're right about the problem. Your job is to find the solution." The Wyatt Cheng rule: of every four pieces of feedback, about two are nothing, one is broken, one is actually broken — act on roughly one of four. Never show a bad build. Golden Cobra's agnostic testing: playtesting without the designer in the room — ideal for a solo dev. Schell: distinguish bug reports — the game crashed — from playtest feedback — the player didn't understand the objective; for a feel pass, weight experience failures over technical failures. Jesse Fish: after reaching submittable state, "show it to as many people as you can" and make a fix or no-fix decision on each note before sleeping.

The techniques — Jazudu's last-mile numbers. Screen shake zero-point-one to zero-point-three seconds, two to five pixel offset, randomized direction each frame, ALWAYS include a settings toggle for motion sickness. Particles five to fifteen per burst max, palette-matched, slight randomness. Hitstop two to four frames on attack connect. White flash one to two frames on damage. Pitch randomize zero-point-nine to one-point-one. Every player action gets at least one sound. Never linear UI motion — ease everything; score counts up, health lerps, menus slide. Layer big-moment sounds — boom plus debris plus bass rumble. Priority: "Start with screen shake, particles, and sound. Those three alone will transform how your game feels." The feedback-budget pattern: a debug overlay summing live feedback load — emitters, peak shake pixels, hit-stop per second, sfx layers — trigger every event at once and verify the peak stays under the High ceiling. Blue Tengu's experiments from Nijman's talk: screen shake via diagonal-alternating object — Awesome; gun kickback — Didn't Use, "looked like a bug" and irritating control removal; enemy knockback — only if tuned. And Jesse Fish's own dev log confession: "Look at adding camera shake then decide it is too much work for the last hour" — feel features need scheduling, not leftover time.

What to avoid. Juicing a bad verb. Spectacle inflation — spending peak effects on routine actions leaves nothing for climaxes. Stale input — don't over-buffer past two hundred milliseconds or you get ghost inputs. Resetting shake per hit — pulsing shake reads as a bug; accumulate and decay instead. Control removal disguised as juice — knockback that steals precise control is irritating, not juicy. No in-game onboarding. No mission select — players who die fifteen minutes in and must restart can't show off your content, especially streamers and judges. The fifty-fifty split violated — half the jam for the game, half for content plus polish plus feedback plus publishing. Features not done by the halfway point get cut.
