Part 2 of 3. Recruit outside the dev's circle — friends and family confirm biases, not findings. The techniques — with numbers. Hit-stop: Jamzo says freeze "for a tiny moment when a big hit lands, around point-oh-five seconds"; used on every minor event it reads as lag. GameDesign.gg: freeze a few frames at impact; Smash Bros. scales hitlag with damage — matching our rarity ladder; warns multi-hit attacks need care or combat turns gluey. Our hit-stop rate cap is exactly the right guard: map thirty-to-eighty-millisecond tiers to the rarity ladder, never global. Screen shake: Jamzo — short and sharp, decay quickly — "a shake should be a punch, not an earthquake"; explicitly an accessibility consideration with a player-facing toggle. GameDesign.gg: "the loudest tool in the juice toolbox, potent in small doses"; scale shake to meaning — "a pistol and a rocket must not" shake the same; "screen shake is punctuation. An exclamation point per sentence is emphasis." Ship intensity toggles. Particles: a handful at the moment of an event — dust on land, sparks on impact — cheap, aims the eye. Our pool is prewarmed and FROZEN — correct architecture for iOS. Easing: run slides through ease-in-out, anticipation before action plus overshoot before settling; squash on landing, stretch at speed; keep direct player movement snappy — easing belongs to non-input motion. Audio: a sound on every meaningful action with pitch varied slightly each time so repetition doesn't grate — our WebAudio synth plus limiter plus ducking already implements this. The Vlambeer reference: Nijman's "Art of Screenshake" stacked about thirty tiny tweaks onto a deliberately flat prototype — almost all the gain was feedback, not content. What to avoid. One: over-juice into unreadable — constant heavy shake makes people queasy and hides the action; five-second particle bursts turn the screen into confetti; hit-stop too long reads as the game freezing; easing on everything makes controls mushy.
