The jam hangar findings. Adam Thompson: set a personal deadline two hours before the real one — the submission hour is when things go wrong; use the buffer for fixes plus icon, screenshots, and a gameplay video. Enigma Dragons: two hours before jam end, create the game page and gameplay video; final hour, test the final build and submit once approved. PIGSquad budgets submitting at about eight percent of a jam — three hours of a forty-eight. The Survival Guide: install libraries, test exporting a small project, sign up for itch in advance — "you definitely don't want to be looking for an exporter during Submission Hour." Thompson: host web builds on itch.io, not Dropbox — bandwidth caps cut public downloads. Don't blank on audio — no audio is N/A or one-star in LD's Audio category, a significant hit to Overall. Post-jam: LD's sorting surfaces games by creator activity — play, rate, comment on other submissions; Thompson rates at least ten games a day. Validation — the five-user protocol. Nielsen and Landauer's math: with an average per-user discovery rate of thirty-one percent, five users surface about eighty-five percent of usability problems. Better: multiple small tests — test, fix, retest. Three rounds of five beats one round of fifteen. NN/g session budgets: sixty to ninety minutes, overhead under eleven percent, focus on observing behavior not soliciting opinions. Bromley's guerrilla protocol: decide objectives as risk to objective to task — "a risk is something you think could be wrong"; find representative players; WATCH people play — watching two or three is more useful than surveying hundreds, and players can't report features they never discovered; trust observed behavior, use opinions only as clues.
