The jam narrative findings. The official LD rules: the Jam is seventy-two hours, teams and individuals, base code and third-party assets allowed — and note, our WebAudio synth is original, so Audio stays contestable, no opt-out. The rating criteria include Humor — "humorous dialog, jokes, and puns" — and Mood — "storytelling, emotion, and the vibe you get while playing." Mood IS the narrative score. Theme is scored separately as a social rule. There's a one-hour Submission Hour after the deadline, then it closes. "The ideal Ludum Dare game can be played right in the browser, in a few minutes, alone." And the Coolness system: playing and rating others raises your game's discoverability. Thompson: work with tools you know; build a warm-up prototype that's submittable BEFORE the jam; his LD28 entry had NO audio — automatic N/A, one-star, a significant overall hit. Brainstorm about two hours at reveal. One unique focus, don't overscope. Finish two hours before the deadline — "the hour where things go most wrong is during submission hour." In those two hours: icon, screenshots, gameplay video. After: rate at least ten games a day, postmortem. Arcweave's forty-eight-hour narrative workflow from a real seventy-two-hour jam with a hundred-twenty participants: the ten-minute rule — "If you can't explain your story in ten minutes, it's too complex for a jam." Three moments, not three acts: a hook that grabs, a choice that defines the character, a payoff that satisfies. One critical path plus max two branches; lock theme integration into that path. Volume over perfection; placeholders for names; top-down polish — most polish on the first third, where most players engage. The three-fix rule: blocking bugs, confusing choices, immersion breaks. "The story works in my tool" does not equal "the export works." "Good enough ships" — a complete fifteen-minute story beats an unfinished masterpiece. Lock content two hours before deadline. Browser instant-play gets significantly higher engagement. Twenty minutes of custom CSS takes a page from prototype to professional.

Validation — Bromley's protocol, the professional basis for our one-shot. Two test types: comprehension — do players understand — and appeal — are they gripped. Recruit from your competitor set; validate narrative interest by having candidates verbally describe story beats that stuck with them from comparable games. Comprehension: one-to-one moderated, slide per story beat, three open questions per beat — "What is happening currently? Why is this happening? What do you expect to happen next?" Never just "is this clear?" Test early at storyboard stage, recheck at milestones. Appeal: regularly ask "how interested are you in what happens next," chart drop-off over time; define the intended emotion per beat and compare against players' word-list picks. The traps: players experience a story as intended only the FIRST time — never reuse playtesters, screen for prior exposure; screen out friends, family, and industry people; do NOT ask for story ideas or "do you like it" — "a recipe for generic low-impact storytelling"; breaks change recall — follow up weeks later on what they remember.

The techniques — Vois on barks that don't grate. A bark is dialogue that survives repetition — "Can't do that" is useful the first time, a personal insult by the end of a puzzle. Separate informational barks from personality lines — warnings must stay legible during combat. For each trigger note frequency, urgency, possible overlap, whether another sound already exists; mark interruptible triggers. Variant counts: three for routine triggers, six for common — a starting exercise. Vary construction — "On my way," "Moving," "I'll get there." More lines don't fix over-triggering — fix cooldowns and priority. Put actionable info first; cut the rest. Set a duration budget per trigger. Test the line while doing the action. Define the character's normal energy plus allowed range; give every line a specific intention — reassure an ally, acknowledge a task, warn the squad. Compare the set side by side — if every variant ends with an emphatic flourish, the pool feels repetitive. And the closer: "Write the line the player needs, then give it permission to stay quiet."

What to avoid. Reusing playtesters — second exposure is contaminated data. Asking "do you like the story" or soliciting ideas. Last-minute features after content lock — the final two hours are for packaging, not content. Dead ends and un-spellchecked openings. Over-scoped branching — more than two branches means an unfinished masterpiece. Missing browser instant-play.
