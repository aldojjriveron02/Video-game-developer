The jam tutorial findings. Thompson's three periods: preparation, execution, networking — "the exercise is mostly in finishing a game." Internal deadline two hours before the real one — "the hour where things go most wrong is during submission hour." Games are rated on Overall, Innovation, Fun, Theme, Graphics, Audio, Humor — missing audio or graphics effort costs a one-star or N/A. After the deadline, rate at least ten games a day to stay findable. Icon, screenshots, and a gameplay video are part of the submission. LD60 is confirmed on the event page: October sixteenth. The jam implication for our tutorial: raters play ten-plus games a day; if a rater can't learn the slice in minutes, they bounce. The tutorial must teach the M3 duel inside the first few minutes, and the game page itself must carry controls and objective so the tutorial never stands alone.

Validation — the Kleenex protocol, confirmed as a real named method. One-time use per test subject; it validates first impressions and initial learning curve. The steps: bring in a fresh player who's never seen the game, observe their first interaction, record where they struggle, and do NOT reuse the same player for this type. The Black Box variant: give the player the game with no instructions beyond basic controls, observe their natural approach, note where assumptions differ from design intent. And the Fundamental Attribution Error corrective: when a tester fails, designers instinctively blame the tester — correct by assuming the game might be at fault. Was the instruction actually clear? Was the icon actually visible? Bromley on numbers: six players when discovering problems — recruit six so you still have five after no-shows; twelve when understanding players; a hundred when measuring. Citing Nielsen: five users surface about eighty percent of issues. "One player is infinitely more than none."

The techniques — Okko's nine-point guide from his Cologne Game Lab thesis. One: a narrative scenario in which teaching makes sense. Two: a mentor character. Three: incorporate the first plot point into the tutorial. Four: reduce action possibilities — add mechanics one at a time. Five: a safe environment for mistakes, at the player's own pace. Six: minimal text — and dub it. Seven: decide what must be explained versus what the player discovers. Eight: different teaching modes per player, and make tutorials skippable. Nine: instructions only when relevant. His thesis game used an NPC pirate mentor that instructs only when the player seems to need it. George Fan's rule from Plants vs. Zombies: never show more than EIGHT WORDS on screen at any moment. Bergdahl's solutions: show don't tell; learn by doing; don't punish early mistakes; adaptive hints driven by actual player behavior; tutorials that are in-game, seamless, fun, and only as long as needed. GMTK on complex games: split the tutorial, spread it across the campaign, use affordances.

What to avoid. Bergdahl's trap list: walls of text; breaking immersion; repeating the same tutorials; dull hologram training; non-canon alternate-reality training arenas; wasting time telling players things they already know; assuming the player's skill level. Okko: unskippable tutorials are a trap in themselves. The community consensus across a hundred-twenty-comment thread and multiple essays: hand-holding, text dumps, unskippable sequences.

Shipped examples. Tom Quinn's LD47 postmortem: designed his entry so simple it required NO tutorial — "people were able to figure out how to play anyway." Half-Life 2 and Portal: invisible tutorials through level design, zero explicit instruction. Celeste: mechanics taught one at a time, then combined. Okko's thesis game: context-sensitive NPC mentor, adaptive and skippable.

The final tutorial decision: TEACH-READY — CONDITIONAL, not signed off. What I implemented this turn: two things. First, George Fan's eight-word rule — our tutorial lines were fourteen and fifteen words; they're now eight and under, in both English and Japanese. "Tap a blue mech, then a highlighted tile." "Tap a red enemy, pick a limb, FIRE." Second, the four pilot assists now default ON for first-time players — never assume skill, per Bergdahl. Veterans can toggle them off; existing saves are unaffected. Zero new lines — just tightened text. The Kleenex Friday protocol is in the spec: six fresh players, each used once, black-box with zero coaching, on iPhone 17 and desktop, every confusion logged as a game fault. Pass criterion: a stranger reaches combat and reads intent telegraphs unaided. Four thousand one hundred seventy-six lines, unchanged.
